Software development is often treated as an individual skill. You learn a programming language, understand a framework, solve problems, and become better at writing code. But the longer you work in software, the more you realize that technical knowledge becomes much more valuable when you can share it with others. That's why every developer should learn to teach.

Teaching doesn't mean standing in front of a classroom. It can be as simple as explaining a piece of code to a teammate, helping a junior developer understand a concept, writing useful documentation, or explaining a technical decision to someone without a technical background. The ability to explain something clearly is a skill in itself.

If you truly understand a concept, you should eventually be able to explain it without making it unnecessarily complicated. Teaching forces you to organize your thoughts, identify what actually matters, and recognize where your own understanding might still have gaps. It's one reason teaching makes developers better.

When you try to explain why a particular architectural decision was made, you may discover that the reasoning isn't as clear as you originally thought. When you explain a piece of code to someone else, you might notice that the implementation could be simpler. Sometimes the person asking the question helps you see something you had stopped noticing.

Teaching also helps teams become less dependent on individual people. If only one developer understands an important part of the system, that knowledge becomes a bottleneck. When developers share what they know, knowledge spreads across the team. People become more confident, collaboration improves, and problems can be solved without always relying on the same person.

There's also an important human side to teaching. Not everyone learns at the same speed or in the same way. A good developer who teaches understands this. They don't make someone feel bad for asking a basic question. They create an environment where curiosity is encouraged, and people feel comfortable saying, “I don't understand this yet.” That kind of environment can have a huge impact on a team.

Teaching doesn't make you less valuable because others learn what you know. Quite the opposite. When your knowledge becomes shared knowledge, the entire team becomes stronger. The best developers are not the ones who keep the most knowledge to themselves.

They are the ones who can turn what they know into something others can understand, use, and eventually teach to someone else. Because in software development, knowledge becomes more valuable when it moves.
