A manager can easily become the person everyone goes to for answers. A decision needs to be made; the manager decides. A problem appears; the manager solves it. Someone gets stuck; the manager steps in.

At first, this can look like strong leadership. The team gets answers quickly, and work keeps moving. But if everything depends on the manager, the team is not becoming stronger. It is becoming dependent.

The best managers understand that their job is not to be the smartest or most important person in the room. Their job is to create an environment where people can make good decisions without constantly needing permission.

That starts with trust.

Independent teams need the freedom to make decisions, experiment with solutions, and occasionally make mistakes. A manager who needs to approve every small decision creates a bottleneck. A manager who provides context, clear expectations, and room to act creates ownership.

This does not mean disappearing and letting the team figure everything out alone. Good managers remain available when guidance is needed. They remove obstacles, provide direction when the situation is unclear, and step in when the team genuinely needs support.

The difference is knowing when not to step in.

An independent team does not mean a team without leadership. It means a team that understands the goal well enough to determine how to get there. Developers can make technical decisions. Product people can make product decisions. Team members can solve problems without waiting for someone higher in the hierarchy to tell them what to do.

This also changes the relationship between managers and their teams. Instead of measuring their value by how many problems they personally solve, great managers measure their impact by how many problems the team can solve without them.

That is a much harder form of leadership. It requires patience. It means allowing someone to take a different approach even when you already know how you would do it. It means asking questions instead of immediately providing answers. It means helping people develop judgment rather than simply giving them instructions.

Over time, something powerful happens. The team becomes more confident. Decisions happen faster. People take greater ownership of their work. Knowledge spreads instead of remaining concentrated in one person. And the manager has more time to focus on the bigger challenges that actually require their attention.

The goal of a great manager is not to make themselves indispensable.

It is to build a team that can succeed even when they are not in the room.

Because the strongest managers don't create followers who constantly look for answers. They create independent people who know how to find them.
